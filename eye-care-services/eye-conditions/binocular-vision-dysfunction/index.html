What Is Binocular Vision Dysfunction?

Binocular vision dysfunction happens when your eyes don’t work well together as a team. Each eye sees the world slightly differently, and your brain must combine those two images into one. When your eyes are misaligned—even by a slight amount—it can disrupt that process. That’s what causes BVD.

Even if your vision is technically “20/20,” binocular dysfunction can still make your eyes work overtime. For many, this leads to strain, double vision, or difficulty focusing, especially during close-up tasks such as reading or working on a computer.

Symptoms of Binocular Vision Dysfunction

Some symptoms of BVD show up in subtle ways and are often mistaken for other eye conditions. Here’s what we frequently see:

For Adults:

Frequent headaches or migraines

Dizziness or lightheadedness

Eye strain, especially with screens

Difficulty driving, particularly at night

Feeling off-balance or unsteady

For Children:

Trouble reading or skipping lines

Poor attention during classroom activities

Avoidance of homework or close-up work

Frequent blinking or eye rubbing

Tilting the head while reading

Unexplained nausea or motion sickness

If your child has been misdiagnosed with ADHD or learning difficulty, it’s worth checking their visual system. Many behaviors linked to focus issues are actually signs of BVD.

What Causes Binocular Vision Dysfunction?

There’s no one-size-fits-all reason. In some cases, BVD shows up after a concussion or mild traumatic brain injury. In others, it’s developmental—something a child is born with and has to grow into (or out of).

For many patients in Texas, we find that it’s a combination of eye-muscle coordination and how the brain interprets visual signals. This dysfunction may not be noticeable until the visual system is under stress—such as during reading, screen time, or sports.
